# Distributed Deployable Photovoltaic Shading Systems — A Technical Roadmap for Urban Heat-Dome-Adaptive Infrastructure

**White Paper | CityShade Project | V1.0 | July 2026**

---

## Abstract

The urban heat dome is an increasingly frequent and seriously underestimated city-scale climate threat under climate change. The superposition of subsiding airflow from high-pressure systems and the urban heat island effect forms a positive feedback loop, driving abnormally high near-surface temperatures in cities and extending their duration from the historical 3–5 days to 10–15 days, placing systemic pressure on public health, energy systems, and economic activity.

Existing responses — air-conditioning expansion, green roofs, high-albedo coatings, fixed PV carports — each carry structural limitations: air-conditioning exhaust heat intensifies the feedback loop; greening is constrained by load-bearing capacity and maintenance; coatings only reduce building heat gain without generating energy; fixed PV lacks dynamic adaptability. Cities urgently need a systemic solution that simultaneously blocks radiative heat gain, converts sunlight into electricity, and imposes no additional energy burden of its own.

This white paper proposes "distributed deployable photovoltaic (PV) shading" as a paradigm for urban thermal-environment-adaptive infrastructure. Its core logic is to intercept the solar shortwave radiation that would otherwise heat the ground and buildings and convert it into electricity, sharply reducing radiative heat gain; the devices themselves form a complete energy loop via PV plus storage, imposing no burden on the grid; and the net thermal effect after deployment is negative. Compared with conventional passive cooling measures such as greening, high-albedo coatings, and evaporative cooling, this approach actively generates electricity while intercepting radiative heat gain, and is reversible, measurable, free of cross-boundary spillover, and economically positive.

Based on engineering design calculations, a single system (leisure-space scenario, 1.4 kWp installed) generates about 4.7 kWh per day in summer with self-consumption of only about 0.3 kWh; the parking-lot scenario (16.5 kWp) generates about 27,375 kWh per year, with a base-case payback of about 3.8 years and an IRR of 18–22%; the building-facade scenario (5 bays / 540 m²) cuts CO₂ by about 63 tonnes per year. At the district scale, for a 100,000-person district at 10% coverage, local peak cooling under the shade reaches about 3–8°C in summer, while the district-average cooling is about 0.05–0.1°C (rising to 0.3–0.5°C at 50% coverage), with annual emission reductions of about 3,000–5,000 tCO₂.

This white paper also notes candidly: the technology route currently lacks large-scale empirical support; the standards system has gaps; cross-departmental approval forms an institutional barrier; and the supply chain for semi-flexible PV modules still needs cultivation. It proposes a phased approach: in the short term (1–2 years), explore standards and pilot validation; in the medium term (3–5 years), explore integration with existing building-energy and municipal-infrastructure standards; and in the long term, explore incorporation into a systemic urban thermal-environment-management framework — directions best discussed jointly with experts and management bodies in the relevant fields.

## Chapter 1 — The Urban Heat Dome: Mechanism, Trends, and Impacts

### 1.1 Physical Mechanism of the Heat Dome

A heat dome forms when a persistent high-pressure system aloft produces subsiding (sinking) airflow that compresses and warms adiabatically, while suppressing cloud formation and precipitation. Over urban areas, this effect couples with the urban heat island (UHI): dense impervious surfaces (asphalt, concrete) absorb and store solar radiation, anthropogenic heat from vehicles and air conditioners adds further load, and the urban canopy geometry traps longwave radiation. The result is a self-reinforcing positive feedback — higher temperatures drive more air-conditioning, whose waste heat raises temperatures further.

### 1.2 Recent Trends: The 2022–2025 Global Heat-Wave Events

The period 2022–2025 saw a marked escalation in extreme heat events globally. Multi-day heat domes affected major regions including North America, Southern Europe, and East Asia, with prolonged durations (10–15 days in some events) and record-breaking peak temperatures, consistent with climate-model projections of increasing frequency, intensity, and duration of extreme heat under continued warming.

### 1.3 Health, Economic, and Energy Impacts

Heat domes impose systemic stress: elevated heat-related mortality and morbidity (especially among outdoor workers and the elderly); reduced labor productivity; accelerated aging of road surfaces (reaching 60–70°C); surging peak electricity demand that strains grids; and increased risk of tire blowouts and infrastructure failure. The economic cost of a single prolonged event can run into billions.

### 1.4 Summary

The urban heat dome is a city-scale, multi-day to multi-week threat that conventional point solutions cannot address. A systemic, deployable, energy-positive intervention is needed.

## Chapter 2 — Existing Responses and Their Limitations

### 2.1 Air-Conditioning Expansion: An Unsustainable Waste-Heat Feedback

Air conditioning cools interiors by rejecting heat outdoors. At city scale during a heat wave, this collectively raises ambient temperature, intensifying the very problem it addresses — a positive feedback loop. It also depends entirely on the grid, precisely when heat waves push demand toward peak.

### 2.2 Green Roofs: Dual Constraints of Load-Bearing and Maintenance

Green roofs provide insulation and evapotranspiration cooling but are limited by roof load capacity, waterproofing integrity, irrigation needs, and ongoing maintenance. Their cooling effect is confined to the building scale and is difficult to scale across dense urban fabric, especially in water-scarce regions.

### 2.3 High-Albedo Coatings: Reduce Building Gain Only, No Generation

Cool roofs and cool pavements reflect solar radiation, reducing surface heat gain, but generate no energy. Reflected light can increase thermal exposure for nearby pedestrians and adjacent buildings, and the approach offers no dynamic adaptability.

### 2.4 Fixed PV Carports: Non-Deployable, No Dynamic Adaptation

Fixed PV carports generate electricity and provide partial shading but cannot adjust to weather conditions; their shading footprint is fixed, and they offer no meteorological adaptability (e.g., stowing against high winds).

### 2.5 Summary: The Missing "Block + Generate + Adapt" Trinity

No existing measure simultaneously blocks radiative heat gain, generates electricity, and dynamically adapts to weather. Distributed deployable PV shading is proposed to fill this gap.

## Chapter 3 — Distributed PV Shading: A Systemic Solution

### 3.1 Core Concept: Convert Intercepted Radiation into Electricity, Not Waste Heat

Rather than merely reflecting or absorbing sunlight, the system intercepts solar shortwave radiation and converts a meaningful fraction directly into electricity. The intercepted energy is thereby diverted from heating the ground and buildings, while producing economic value.

### 3.2 Comparison with Existing Urban Cooling Measures

Distributed PV shading does not replace existing measures but offers a differentiated complement along the dimensions of active energy generation, reversibility/measurability, and economic return.

| Measure | Cooling Mechanism | Key Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Air-conditioning expansion | Indoor cooling | Waste heat rejected outdoors intensifies UHI and the heat-dome feedback; grid-dependent |
| Urban greening (trees/parks) | Shade + evapotranspiration | Long growth cycle, limited coverage, high maintenance, hard in water-scarce regions |
| Green roofs | Roof insulation + evapotranspiration | Limited by roof load and waterproofing; complex maintenance; building-scale only |
| High-albedo coatings | Reflect solar radiation | Reduces building/pavement gain only, no generation; reflected light may raise nearby exposure |
| Evaporative cooling (mist/water) | Phase-change absorption | Water- and energy-intensive; raises humidity; unsustainable in arid regions |
| Fixed PV carports/rooftop PV | Generation + partial shade | Not dynamically adjustable; no meteorological adaptability; fixed shading footprint |
| **Distributed deployable PV shading** | **Intercept radiation and convert to electricity** | **Shading and generation integrated; deployable to weather; reversible, measurable, economically positive** |

The core distinction: it does not merely "reduce heat gain" but converts radiation that would heat the city into electricity of economic value, making the cooling action itself yield a positive return — a "no-regret" intervention whose generation and carbon benefits hold even if cooling falls short of expectations.

### 3.3 Self-Powered Closed Loop: No Burden on the Grid

Each system pairs PV with integrated storage to form a self-contained energy loop. Sensing, control, and actuation are powered by the system's own generation, so deployment adds no net demand to the grid.

### 3.4 The Case for a Negative Net Thermal Effect

A common question: PV modules intercept 80–95% of incoming shortwave radiation but convert only 15–22% to electricity — does the remaining 60–80% re-enter the urban heat cycle?

**Basic premise — energy conservation and "fate control."** The intercepted, non-electrical energy is ultimately released as heat and cannot be eliminated. "Negative net thermal effect" therefore does not mean total energy decreases (energy is conserved) but that **the heat entering the near-surface pedestrian zone is sharply reduced**, while part of the energy leaves the site as electricity. The key is changing the **location, form, and timing** of heat release — the very mechanism by which PV shading cools relative to unshaded ground.

**Three fates of the intercepted radiation:**

**(1) Electricity export — true removal (~15–22%).** The converted electricity is carried off-site via DC busbars and consumed elsewhere, physically leaving the local environment — the only genuinely "removed" portion. Storage peak-shifting (shifting daytime-captured energy to night use) and active cooling powered by self-generated electricity (ventilation, misting, cold storage) are extensions of this pathway, not separate fates. If the electricity displaces fossil generation, it further avoids distant waste heat.

**(2) Longwave radiation directed to the sky (~30–40%).** Heated to ~50–65°C, modules re-radiate in the longwave infrared. Longwave cannot pass through glass, so the upward portion escapes through the atmospheric window (8–13 μm) toward the ~3 K space sink (passive radiative cooling); the downward portion is reflected/blocked by a glass or high-reflectivity backsheet, reducing downwelling heat into the shaded zone.

**(3) Convective heat lifted to the upper boundary layer (~20–30%).** Modules heat adjacent air, forming rising thermal plumes that enter the upper boundary layer and are diluted by ambient wind. Because the release point is at device height (carport/canopy top) rather than ground level, the effect on the sub-2 m pedestrian zone is limited. Elevated mounting and under-module ventilation strengthen this.

**The key difference from unshaded ground — release location.** Unshaded, ~85% of incoming shortwave reaches asphalt and building surfaces directly, converting to heat at pedestrian height (0–2 m), heating the ground, which then heats the air — the core of the UHI. PV shading intercepts this radiation aloft, raising the release point from ground to device height and redistributing it via the three pathways above.

**Quantitative example (parking-lot scenario, incoming = 100%):**

| Fate | Share of incoming | Note | Enters urban heat cycle? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Converted to electricity and exported | ~18% | At 18% module efficiency; carried off-site, the only true removal | Fully removed |
| Longwave upward to sky | ~35% | Escapes via atmospheric window; longwave cannot pass glass | To the sky sink |
| Convection to upper boundary layer | ~12% | Rising plumes diluted aloft; limited sub-2 m effect | Diluted aloft |
| Longwave downward + near-surface residual | ~20% | Downwelling longwave (after backsheet reflection) + minor near-ground exchange | Still affects near-surface |
| System self-consumption | <1% | Controllers/sensors/actuators; 2–6% of generation, negligible vs. incoming | Negligible |

By contrast, unshaded asphalt converts ~85% of incoming radiation into ground heat locally. PV shading removes ~18% as electricity and routes ~47% (upward longwave + convection) to the sky and upper air, leaving only ~20% affecting the near-surface pedestrian zone. The near-surface heat load thus falls from ~85% (unshaded) to ~20% — a reduction of roughly three-quarters. This is the substance of "negative net thermal effect."

**Note:** The shares above are engineering estimates for a typical summer peak (module ~55°C, wind ~1–2 m/s, elevated mounting); absolute values vary with wind, module temperature, mounting, and sky view factor, but the fate-partition framework is robust. Values await measurement or high-fidelity simulation.

In sum, the net thermal effect is negative: heat entering the urban heat cycle is far less than the intercepted-and-diverted portion. Higher deployment density, better convective conditions, and higher backsheet reflectivity all strengthen local cooling. The analysis rests on engineering calculation and physical reasoning and awaits large-scale empirical validation; Chapter 5 gives quantitative estimates and Chapter 6 discusses the data gap.

## Chapter 4 — Technical Architecture and Scenario Adaptation

> **Chapter takeaways:** Distributed PV shading adopts a modular platform architecture, achieving cross-scenario interchange and combination through standardized three-tier interfaces (mechanical / electrical / communications). Five scenarios — leisure space, slow-traffic roads, fast-traffic roads, building sun-facing facades, and parking lots — share the same control architecture and safety logic while adapting in deployment form, installed power, and structural system. Among them, the parking lot is promoted first as a mature, scalably replicable PV-carport form, with deployability/foldability as an optional enhancement for extreme weather. Key technical features include deployability, adjustable translucency, safety prioritization, and autonomous fail-safe on power loss.

### 4.1 Modular Platform Architecture

The architecture follows a "platform + module" design. The system comprises five functional modules:

1. **Shading-PV integrated module:** PV material directly forms the shading surface, with material type selected by scenario (crystalline full-shade, semi-transparent thin-film, fully flexible PV fabric, semi-flexible modules, adjustable PV louvers).
2. **Structural deployment module:** realizes unfolding and stowing of the shading surface, in four forms — umbrella-petal, zigzag folding, single-roller multi-stage lowering, and space tubular truss.
3. **Storage-electrical module:** three-tier DC busbars (48V/512V/800V) match different power levels; standardized quick-swap storage interfaces enable rapid replacement.
4. **Sensing-control module:** multi-sensor fusion (wind speed, irradiance, temperature, rain/snow, pedestrian/vehicle radar) + three-tier communications architecture (management / control / sensing layers), protocol-agnostic.
5. **Mobile/fixed foundation module:** counterweight, helical ground anchor, or cast-in-place concrete, selectable.

The five modules connect via standardized three-tier interfaces (mechanical, electrical, communications), enabling cross-scenario interchange and combination. The same control system fits four deployment forms; the same safety logic covers five scenarios.

The core value: rather than designing a separate system per scenario, it builds a composable, extensible platform. New scenarios need only develop an adapted structural-deployment module; the rest is reused.

### 4.1.1 Industrial-Base Advantage: Leveraging Domestic PV Capacity

China has the world's most complete and largest PV supply chain — polysilicon, wafer, cell, and module capacity each account for over 80% of the global total, making it one of the few clean-energy equipment sectors with full-chain domestic supply. This provides a distinctive industrial-base advantage for large-scale deployment:

1. **Cost advantage:** Domestic module prices have fallen to historic lows (mainstream modules around ¥0.7–0.9/W in 2024–2025, with further downside), giving the generation portion strong cost competitiveness and sharply reducing dependence on subsidies.
2. **Supply-chain resilience:** Full-chain domestic supply from polysilicon to modules carries no import dependence or geopolitical disruption risk, aligning naturally with the localization and controllability that urban infrastructure demands.
3. **Industrial synergy:** Urban PV shading opens a new load-center market for domestic PV capacity, helping smooth capacity cycles and forming a two-way positive feedback between "urban cooling demand" and "PV capacity absorption."

This strategic advantage should be reflected in policy discussion and project communication (see Chapter 7).

### 4.2 Five Scenarios in Brief

**(1) Leisure space (umbrella-petal deployment)**

Urban plazas, residential courtyards, and parks are vital daily-activity spaces and among the highest heat-exposure zones during heat domes. Typically lacking fixed shade, residents are forced to cut outdoor activity during heat waves. Umbrella PV shading uses a central mast plus radial arms, deploying on demand to provide shade while generating electricity for lighting, charging, and other public services. Its mobility allows flexible placement per activity needs.

**Engineering structure:** A central steel mast (flange + anchor bolts) carries radial aluminum-alloy arms, between which flexible PV film is tensioned to form the canopy; electric actuators/winches drive the arms synchronously via a central drive disk for petal-style deployment. Foundations are selectable — prefabricated counterweight bases for temporary placement, helical anchors for semi-permanent deployment. Canopy tilt and arm count are set by target shade area and wind-load cases.

**(2) Slow-traffic roads (sidewalks / non-motorized lanes)**

Sidewalks and bike lanes carry slow urban traffic and are the main heat-exposure setting for pedestrians and cyclists during heat domes. Street trees offer limited, slow-maturing shade; fixed canopies lack generation. Zigzag folding-grille PV shading deploys linearly along the road, unfolding by day into a continuous shade corridor and stowing to the post side at night or in high wind, preserving traffic clearance and streetscape.

**Engineering structure:** Steel posts are set along the roadside at span intervals; zigzag hinged folding grilles cantilever from the post tops, each span comprising multiple grille units hinged in series, driven laterally by electric lead screws/racks. In the deployed state, semi-transparent PV modules on the grille balance shading and daylight. Foundations use helical anchors or shallow concrete pile caps, accommodating linear placement and utility avoidance; stowed, the mechanism nests against the post to preserve road clear width and visual openness.

**(3) Fast-traffic roads (motor-vehicle lanes / expressways)**

Expressway asphalt absorbs heavy solar radiation in summer, reaching 60–70°C, accelerating pavement aging and raising tire-blowout risk. Space tubular-truss PV shading spans lanes as a gantry, shading the pavement while generating electricity for roadside monitoring, lighting, and communications, replacing conventional cable runs.

**Engineering structure:** Twin posts plus a top space tubular truss form a portal span; PV modules on the truss chords create a continuous shading roof, with span set by lane count and structure gauge to meet clearance requirements. Cast-in-place deep-pile foundations resist high wind load with vehicle-impact safety margin; generated power is aggregated along a DC busbar for monitoring, lighting, and communications, reducing external cabling.

**(4) Building sun-facing facades (single-roller multi-stage lowering)**

South/west facades absorb heavy solar radiation in summer, a major source of cooling load. Conventional external shading (fixed louvers, awnings) lacks generation and dynamic adjustment. Single-roller multi-stage lowering PV shading unrolls a flexible PV curtain downward from a roof roller, selecting among 6 m / 12 m / 18 m staging positions per solar altitude and indoor load, balancing shading coverage and daylight.

**Engineering structure:** An electric roller mechanism at the roof eave lowers the flexible PV curtain along side guide rails/cables, with mechanical limit stops at the three stages; curtain self-weight plus a bottom weight bar maintains tension, while rails restrain wind flutter. The connection to the building structure must be verified for roof load capacity, with auxiliary steel beams added where needed; wiring runs concealed along the facade, integrating with building storage/distribution.

**(5) Parking lots (fixed portal rigid frame + optional typhoon-hinged folding)**

Parking lots are among the largest hardened open spaces in cities; summer vehicle baking pushes cabin temperatures above 70°C while asphalt absorbs heavy radiation. Among the five scenarios, the parking lot has the highest technical maturity, the clearest business model, and the strongest conditions for first-mover scaled rollout: a portal rigid-frame PV carport provides fixed parking shade while generating electricity directly for charging piles, forming an integrated "PV + storage + charging" loop with a clear payback path and mature engineering practice in multiple locales.

**Engineering structure:** A portal rigid frame (H-section steel posts + double-/single-slope roof beams) is the primary load-bearing structure, each bay covering 1–2 parking spaces; the roof is fully clad at a fixed tilt with high-efficiency crystalline modules for full shading and generation. Cast-in-place independent column foundations are designed for local wind, snow, and seismic combinations; electrical configuration follows "self-consumption with surplus to grid," with DC aggregation then local absorption via storage and charging piles.

**Optional enhancement — typhoon-hinged folding:** In typhoon- and high-wind-prone regions, hinged folding posts can be added to the portal frame. On typhoon warning, the posts drive the roof to fold to a minimum windward angle, sharply reducing wind load to avoid structural damage, then resetting after the wind passes. This is an optional configuration for extreme weather, not the core selling point of the parking-lot solution; in non-typhoon regions, the fixed portal frame is the economically rational default.

### 4.3 Key Technical Features

**(1) Deployability.** Unlike fixed PV, deployable design lets the system adjust dynamically to weather — stowing in high wind to reduce windward area, deploying in sun to maximize shading and generation. This means wind design need not target the worst case (50-year wind load), reducing structural redundancy and cost.

**(2) Adjustable translucency.** Scenarios differ in translucency needs: leisure spaces need moderate translucency for comfort, parking lots need full shading to maximize generation, and facades need dynamic balance of shading and daylight. The system matches this via PV materials of differing translucency (80–95% shading) and adjustable louver mechanisms.

**(3) Safety prioritization.** A unified safety-event priority hierarchy is defined: emergency stop (P0) > wind-speed exceedance (P1) > fire alarm (P2) > storage thermal-runaway warning (P3) > pedestrian/vehicle radar intrusion (P4) > rain/snow trigger (P5) > communications loss (P6) > automatic policy (P7) > manual remote command (P8). Higher-priority events preempt lower ones; manual commands cannot block safety-critical policies.

**(4) Autonomous fail-safe on power loss.** The system uses its own PV plus integrated storage to form a closed-loop autonomous fail-safe. On grid loss, integrated storage takes over, driving the mechanism to a safe position and mechanically locking it. For the slow-traffic scenario, a 48V/100Ah (4.8 kWh) pack uses about 25 Wh per stow — only 0.5% of capacity — enabling over 100 safe actions (engineering estimate). When storage is unavailable, lead-screw self-locking / electromagnetic brakes provide the final backstop.

## Chapter 5 — Quantitative Assessment

> **Chapter takeaways:** Based on engineering design calculations, a single system (leisure-space scenario) generates 4.7 kWh/day in summer with only 0.3 kWh self-consumption; the parking-lot scenario generates about 27,375 kWh/year with a base-case payback of about 3.8 years and IRR of 18–22%; the building-facade scenario (5 bays / 540 m²) cuts CO₂ by about 63 tonnes/year. District-scale scenario simulation shows that for a 100,000-person district at 10% coverage, local peak cooling under the shade reaches about 3–8°C in summer and district-average cooling about 0.05–0.1°C (rising to 0.3–0.5°C at 50% coverage), with annual emission reductions of about 3,000–5,000 tonnes. Emphasis: these figures rest on engineering calculation and physical reasoning and await large-scale empirical validation.

### 5.1 Single-System Energy Balance

For the leisure-space scenario (1.4 kWp installed), summer daily generation is about 4.7 kWh against self-consumption of only about 0.3 kWh (sensing, control, actuation), leaving ample net surplus. The parking-lot scenario (16.5 kWp) generates about 27,375 kWh/year.

### 5.2 District-Scale Deployment Scenario Simulation

To assess district-scale cooling potential, two spatial scales must be distinguished — **local scale** (the shaded zone and adjacent street canyon) and **district scale** (full-area average). The two differ by an order of magnitude and must not be conflated: what residents actually feel, and what is most communicable, is local cooling; the district average is diluted by unshaded area and is much smaller, scaling approximately linearly with coverage.

**Assumptions:**
- District population: 100,000
- District area: ~15 km² (at 150 m² per capita)
- Deployment coverage: 10% (i.e., 1.5 km² of shading, base case)
- Scenario mix: parking 40% + slow-traffic roads 30% + building facades 20% + leisure space 10%
- Weighted average installed power density: ~150 Wp/m²

**Estimates (engineering calculation and physical reasoning):**

| Metric | Estimate | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Total installed power | ~22.5 MWp | 1.5 km² × 150 Wp/m² |
| Annual generation | ~22.5 GWh | At 1,000 equivalent full-load hours/year |
| Annual intercepted radiant heat | ~1.35×10⁵ GJ | At 85% shading, annual incident radiation |
| Annual emission reduction | ~3,000–5,000 tCO₂ | At grid factor 0.6 kgCO₂/kWh |
| Local cooling (shaded zone, summer peak) | ~3–8°C | Near-surface air; surface-temperature drop larger |
| District-average cooling (10% coverage) | ~0.05–0.1°C | Steady-state energy balance; scales linearly with coverage |

**Local cooling estimate:** The shading surface intercepts ~85% of incoming shortwave, of which ~18% becomes electricity and the rest dissipates as heat, reducing the covered zone's radiative gain to ~15% of original. At summer peak, ground temperature under the shade drops ~10–20°C versus exposed areas, and near-surface air temperature drops ~3–8°C (engineering radiation-budget calculation and analogy with comparable shading/greening studies). This is the cooling residents actually feel and the most direct climate-adaptation value.

**Steady-state model for district-average cooling:** Cooling contribution is expressed as continuous thermal power. The 1.5 km² of shading reduces summer daily heat gain by ~2.13×10⁷ MJ, equivalent to a continuous power of ~247 MW; spread over the 15 km² district, this is a heat-flux reduction of ~16.5 W/m². Using a simplified 1-D steady-state boundary-layer model ΔT ≈ q·τ/(ρ·Cp·H), with convective exchange timescale τ ≈ 1 h, mixing-layer height H = 500 m, air density ρ = 1.2 kg/m³, and specific heat Cp = 1.005 kJ/(kg·K), district-average cooling is ~0.05°C (0.05–0.15°C for τ = 1–3 h).

Note: the earlier "static air-column instantaneous warming" method (ΔT = Q/(ρ·Cp·V)) treated the district atmosphere as a sealed box, ignoring advection and convective exchange; its instantaneous result cannot be accumulated, so this version adopts the steady-state method above.

**Coverage sensitivity:** Under the steady-state model, district-average cooling scales approximately linearly with coverage:

| Coverage | Shaded area | District-average cooling (summer daily) |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | 1.5 km² | ~0.05–0.1°C |
| 20% | 3.0 km² | ~0.1–0.2°C |
| 30% | 4.5 km² | ~0.2–0.3°C |
| 50% | 7.5 km² | ~0.3–0.5°C |

This shows that significant district cooling is the result of scaled deployment — only at 30–50% coverage does the full-area average exceed 0.3°C. Far from a weakness, this is the core argument for whole-district scaled rollout rather than scattered token deployment.

**Caliber clarification:** What residents feel is local cooling under the shade (3–8°C); the district average is a full-area-diluted metric. The two must not be confused. Communication should lead with local cooling and present the district average as secondary, labeled with its coverage.

**Limitations:** The estimates rest on a steady-state simplified model; parameters such as convective exchange timescale and mixing-layer height carry uncertainty, and advection, cloud cover, seasonality, and shading layout are not fully resolved. The local 3–8°C is a typical range from engineering calculation and analogy; actual magnitude varies by site and weather. Conclusions await measurement or high-fidelity simulation (CFD / urban canopy modeling).

### 5.3 Carbon-Reduction Quantification

At a grid emission factor of 0.6 kgCO₂/kWh, the parking-lot scenario avoids about 16.4 tCO₂/year; the building-facade scenario (5 bays / 540 m²) about 63 tCO₂/year. District-scale at 10% coverage avoids about 3,000–5,000 tCO₂/year.

### 5.4 Economic Model

The parking-lot scenario shows a base-case payback of about 3.8 years and IRR of 18–22%; the building-facade scenario about 4 years; the leisure-space scenario about 1.3 years. Levelized cost is above utility-scale PV but below commercial/industrial tariffs; adding air-conditioning savings and carbon-credit value, comprehensive return per kWh is attractive. Sensitivity to module price, tariff, and utilization is moderate within plausible ranges.

## Chapter 6 — Key Challenges for Scaled Deployment

### 6.1 Standards Gap

No dedicated standards yet exist for deployable PV shading systems — covering product classification, structural safety (deployed/stowed wind load, seismic, snow), electrical safety (PV + storage + charging integration), control and safety logic, and performance test methods. The absence of standards constrains market access and insurer confidence.

### 6.2 Approval Barriers

Such projects span multiple management domains. In current practice, for example: transport departments approve structures within highway building-control zones (expressway scenarios); housing/construction departments handle facade-attachment planning permits and building-energy review; and further touchpoints involve urban management, fire, and power. Cross-domain coordination raises transaction costs.

### 6.3 Supply-Chain Constraints

Semi-flexible and fully flexible PV modules — needed for several deployment forms — are not yet mature in mass production, with limited suppliers and higher cost than rigid modules. Supply-chain cultivation is a prerequisite for several scenarios.

### 6.4 Operations & Maintenance Model

Long-term O&M — fatigue life of deployable mechanisms, corrosion, performance under extreme weather, cleaning, and storage degradation — lacks proven service models. Who owns, operates, and maintains distributed shading assets at city scale remains an open question.

### 6.5 Limitations of the Assessment

It must be stated clearly: the cooling estimates, economic analysis, and carbon quantification in this white paper all rest on engineering design calculation and physical reasoning, and have not been validated by large-scale empirical data. Specifically:

- **Cooling effect:** Local cooling of 3–8°C and district-average cooling (~0.05–0.1°C at 10% coverage) rest on a steady-state energy-balance simplified model that does not fully resolve real urban morphology, ventilation corridors, vegetation, advection, and spatial layout.
- **Economics:** Payback estimates rest on assumed tariffs, carbon-credit prices, and utilization; actual returns may deviate materially with site conditions, operating efficiency, and policy changes.
- **Reliability:** Long-term reliability of deployable mechanisms (fatigue life, corrosion, extreme-weather performance) has not been validated in operation.
- **User acceptance:** Public and managerial acceptance of "deployable structures over roads" remains uncertain.

These data gaps are not grounds to reject the route but define the direction of next steps. Pilots and empirical research are the necessary path to closing them.

## Chapter 7 — Policy Considerations and Directions for Action

> **Chapter takeaways:** Scaled deployment of distributed PV shading depends on the progressive maturation of supporting conditions in standards, approval, and industry. This chapter sets out, by short term (1–2 years), medium term (3–5 years), and long term, directions and topics worth considering, for discussion with experts and management bodies in the relevant fields.

### 7.1 Short Term (1–2 years): Standards Exploration and Pilot Validation

This phase is best focused on foundational "from-zero-to-one" topics:

- **Standards framework:** It is worth exploring, with relevant industry associations and standards bodies, a standards framework for "deployable PV shading systems," covering product classification and naming, structural and electrical safety, control and safety logic, and performance test methods — building experience for market access and later standards upgrades.
- **Pilots:** Representative scenarios (parking lots, leisure spaces, building facades) are worth piloting at small scale, with monitoring of temperature, irradiance, generation, energy use, and user feedback, to build empirical data on techno-economics, public acceptance, and O&M models.
- **Linkage with existing policy directions:** Building on existing policy directions such as climate-adaptive city initiatives, it is worth exploring the feasibility of treating distributed PV shading as one technical route for urban thermal-environment-adaptive infrastructure.

### 7.2 Medium Term (3–5 years): Standards Integration and Mechanism Coordination

As pilot experience accumulates, the following directions are worth further discussion with experts and management bodies:

- **Integration with current standards:** How to incorporate design, energy-contribution accounting, and review requirements for deployable PV shading into the revision of relevant building-energy and urban-road engineering standards.
- **Approval coordination:** Such projects often span construction, transport, urban management, fire, and power; feasible cross-domain coordination approaches are worth exploring to reduce institutional cost.
- **Supply-chain cultivation:** Industrialization and cost reduction of semi-flexible and fully flexible PV modules depend on sustained industry investment; corresponding industrial-synergy pathways are worth exploring.
- **Economic assessment:** As costs fall and carbon constraints tighten, project payback and comprehensive returns warrant ongoing assessment to inform scaled decisions.

### 7.3 Long Term: Incorporation into Urban Thermal-Environment Management

Over a longer horizon, the following directions are worth exploring:

- **Systemic planning:** Incorporating distributed PV shading alongside ventilation corridors, greening systems, and high-albedo surfaces into a systemic urban thermal-environment-adaptive management framework.
- **Monitoring and evaluation:** Leveraging urban thermal-environment monitoring networks to support siting, deployment-density optimization, and effect evaluation.
- **Realizing carbon-reduction value:** Exploring feasible pathways for project emission reductions to participate in voluntary-reduction and other market mechanisms, strengthening project economics.

---

## Conclusion

Distributed deployable PV shading proposes a new urban-infrastructure paradigm: converting intercepted solar radiation into electricity rather than waste heat, with the devices themselves forming a complete energy loop via PV plus storage, and a negative net thermal effect after deployment. Compared with existing urban cooling measures such as greening, high-albedo coatings, and evaporative cooling, it is reversible, measurable, free of cross-boundary spillover, and economically positive — a "no-regret" intervention.

Based on engineering calculation, the route is already economically viable: the parking-lot scenario shows a payback of about 3.8 years and IRR of 18–22%; the building-facade scenario about 4 years; the leisure-space scenario about 1.3 years. District-scale simulation shows that at 10% coverage, local peak cooling under the shade reaches about 3–8°C in summer and district-average cooling about 0.05–0.1°C (rising to 0.3–0.5°C at 50% coverage).

It must be noted, however, that these figures rest on engineering calculation and physical reasoning, without large-scale empirical support. Standards gaps, approval barriers, supply-chain constraints, and O&M models are the key challenges for scaled deployment.

We believe it is worth considering standards exploration and pilot validation as the starting point, integration with building-energy and municipal-infrastructure standards as the medium-term direction, and incorporation into an urban thermal-environment-adaptive management framework as the long-term vision — discussing jointly with experts and management bodies in the relevant fields the feasible path to industrialization and scaled application of this technical route.
